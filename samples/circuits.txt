An electric current is defined as the rate of flow of electric charges across any cross section of a conductor. In metallic wires, electrons constitute the flow of electric charges because free electrons drift when an electric potential difference is maintained by a chemical cell or battery. By convention, the direction of electric current is considered opposite to the direction of the flow of electrons, moving from the positive terminal towards the negative terminal. The continuous and closed conducting path along which an electric current flows is called an electric circuit. When a circuit is broken or a switch is turned off, the current stops flowing instantly. The electrical potential difference between two points is measured in volts using an instrument called a voltmeter, which is connected in parallel across the electrical component. According to Ohm's law, the electric current passing through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its terminals, provided the temperature remains constant. Electric resistance measures the opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of electric current.
